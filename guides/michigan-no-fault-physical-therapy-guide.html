The Michigan No-Fault Physical Therapy Guide

Every rule that matters — visit caps, who pays, the one-year-back rule, and your rights — explained in plain English.

Home › Guides › The Michigan No-Fault Physical Therapy Guide

Published Oct 2026 · Editorial fact-check against Michigan statute and official sources · Clinical review pending · General information, not medical or legal advice

If you were hurt in a Michigan car accident, your physical therapy is almost certainly covered — more generously than most people, and frankly many clinics, understand. This guide explains the system in plain English: who pays, what's covered, the deadlines that actually matter, and the mistakes that cost injured people their benefits.

Who pays: your own insurance, regardless of fault

Michigan is a no-fault state. After an accident, your own auto insurer pays your medical costs through Personal Injury Protection (PIP) benefits — even if the crash was entirely the other driver's fault, and even if it was yours. Fault matters for vehicle damage and lawsuits; it does not decide who pays for your treatment.

Which policy pays follows a priority order: generally your own policy first, then a spouse's or resident relative's, and — for people with no household policy — the Michigan Assigned Claims Plan (MAIPF). Passengers, pedestrians, and cyclists injured by cars are covered too. If you're unsure which policy applies, a clinic that knows no-fault (or an attorney) can sort it in minutes.

Is there a visit limit? No — the standard is 'reasonably necessary'

Unlike commercial health plans that cap therapy at 20–60 visits a year, Michigan no-fault has no set visit cap. The legal standard is whether care is reasonably necessary for your accident injuries. Your plan of care should be driven by documented medical need — measured progress, functional goals — not an arbitrary number. Insurers can and do review utilization, which is why objective documentation (measured range of motion, strength, function) protects you.

What does it cost you? Usually nothing

PIP medical benefits pay providers under Michigan's no-fault fee schedule. For covered, reasonably necessary care, injured patients typically pay nothing out of pocket. One nuance since 2020: drivers now choose PIP coverage levels ($50,000 for some Medicaid enrollees, $250,000, $500,000, or unlimited). Most injuries never approach even the lower caps, but your election matters — and it's worth knowing before you need it.

The deadlines that actually matter

  • One year to claim: written notice of injury generally must reach the insurer within 1 year of the accident.
  • The one-year-back rule: benefits are only recoverable for expenses incurred within 1 year before a lawsuit is filed — in practice, providers must bill promptly and disputes must not be left to age. A clinic that sits on billing can forfeit your coverage.
  • No deadline on recovery itself: if your condition flares two years after the crash and care is reasonably necessary and causally related, it's still claimable — with the documentation burden rising as time passes.

Symptoms that show up days later are normal — and covered

Whiplash and soft-tissue symptoms commonly appear 24–72 hours after a collision, once adrenaline fades and inflammation develops. Delayed onset doesn't weaken coverage, but delayed evaluation weakens both recovery and the record. The practical rule: any post-crash pain gets evaluated within the week.

Do you need a lawyer to get treatment?

No. PIP benefits are yours by statute, and treatment should start on the claim, not on a settlement. Attorneys earn their place when insurers delay, cut off, or dispute care — and a good clinic's objective documentation is exactly what makes those disputes winnable. (Clinics and attorneys are prohibited from paying each other for referrals; if you encounter that arrangement, walk away.)

What good no-fault treatment looks like

  • Claim verified before the first visit — no surprise about who pays
  • Objective baseline testing (instrumented range-of-motion and strength measurement), not just 'patient reports pain'
  • A plan of care with functional goals and re-measurement, so necessity is documented, not asserted
  • Prompt billing within the one-year-back window
  • Communication with your physician, adjuster, and — if you have one — your attorney
