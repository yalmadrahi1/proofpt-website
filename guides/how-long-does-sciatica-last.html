How Long Does Sciatica Last? (And How to Sleep With It)

A realistic recovery timeline, what makes sciatica drag on versus settle quickly, the sleep positions that actually help, and when to get it looked at.

Home › Guides › How Long Does Sciatica Last

Published Nov 2026 · Editorial fact-check against clinical practice guidelines · Clinically reviewed by Zubair Fayyaz, PT, DPT · General information, not medical advice

The good news first: most sciatica gets better. The shooting leg pain that makes sitting, sleeping, and standing miserable usually eases within weeks — and how you move and sleep in the meantime has a real effect on how fast.

Most sciatica improves within a few weeks and resolves within about 6–12 weeks with active care. Leg symptoms often start retreating toward the back within 2–3 weeks. A minority lasts beyond three months. Staying active, doing movements that reduce the leg symptoms, and sleeping in positions that unload the nerve speed recovery; bed rest tends to prolong it.

How long does sciatica last?

Sciatica isn't a diagnosis on its own — it's nerve pain, usually from a lumbar disc or a narrowed space pressing on a nerve root, that travels from the low back down the leg. Its timeline usually falls into three stages:

  • Acute (first ~4 weeks) — often the most intense. Many people improve substantially in this window with the right activity and movement.
  • Subacute (4–12 weeks) — steady improvement for most; the leg pain shrinks back toward the buttock and back, and function returns.
  • Chronic (beyond 12 weeks) — a minority. It's not a dead end, but it usually needs a structured physical therapy program rather than waiting.

A useful early sign is centralization: when the pain retreats up the leg toward the spine, recovery is usually on track, even if the back feels sorer for a bit. Pain spreading further down the leg is the opposite signal and worth an evaluation.

What makes sciatica last longer

  • Prolonged sitting and bed rest — the single most common self-inflicted delay.
  • High pain intensity or numbness/weakness early on.
  • Avoiding all movement out of fear, which stiffens everything around the nerve.
  • Sleep loss and high stress, which lower pain tolerance.

Most of these are changeable — which is why early, guided activity beats rest.

How to sleep with sciatica

Night is often the worst part: lying still removes the gentle movement that eases nerve pressure, and some positions load the irritated nerve. What helps most people:

  • On your side with a pillow between the knees — keeps the pelvis and spine aligned so the nerve isn't stretched. Lying on the pain-free side is often more comfortable.
  • On your back with a pillow under the knees — takes tension off the low back and nerve.
  • A loose fetal position helps some people with disc-related sciatica by opening space for the nerve.
  • Avoid sleeping on your stomach, which arches the low back.
  • Get in and out of bed by rolling as one unit — bend the knees, roll shoulders and hips together, and push up with the arms rather than twisting or folding forward.
  • If you wake in pain, a minute of gentle position change or a short walk often resets it better than lying and bracing.

There's no single "correct" position — the best one is whatever keeps you comfortable enough to sleep. Use these as starting points.

What speeds recovery

The evidence is consistent: stay active within the limits of your pain. Gentle walking, frequent position changes, and specific movements that make the leg symptoms smaller are part of healing. A physical therapy plan for sciatica finds the movements that centralize your symptoms, builds back and hip strength so it's less likely to return, and keeps you moving while the nerve calms down. Not sure it's sciatica at all? See herniated disc vs. sciatica and SI joint pain, which can mimic it.

Red flags — get help now

Go to an emergency department for any of these: loss of bowel or bladder control, numbness in the saddle/groin area, or rapidly worsening weakness in a leg. These are rare but urgent. Also get evaluated for severe night pain with unexplained weight loss or fever.

Sciatica questions, answered

How long does sciatica usually take to heal?

Most cases improve within a few weeks and resolve within about 6–12 weeks with active care; leg symptoms often start centralizing within 2–3 weeks. A minority lasts beyond three months and benefits from a structured program.

What is the best sleeping position for sciatica?

Side-lying with a pillow between the knees, or on your back with a pillow under the knees, suits most people; some with disc-related sciatica prefer a loose fetal position. Avoid stomach sleeping, and roll as one unit getting in and out of bed.

Should I rest or keep moving?

Keep moving within your pain limits — prolonged bed rest tends to slow recovery. Gentle walking and movements that reduce the leg symptoms help.

Why does sciatica hurt more at night?

Lying still reduces the movement that eases nerve pressure, some positions load the nerve, and there's less to distract from the pain. Position changes and a short gentle movement often help.
