Post-Surgical Rehabilitation in Sterling Heights, MI

Recovery after orthopedic surgery, planned around your procedure, your surgeon's instructions, and your own assessed starting point — and measured as you go. Opening February 2027 on Mound Rd.

Home › Services › Post-Surgical Rehabilitation
The short version: surgery repairs the structure; rehabilitation rebuilds the motion, strength, and confidence you use it with. At Proof, your plan follows your surgeon's protocol and precautions, starts from a measured baseline, and is adjusted as you progress. There is no one-size recovery — the right pace depends on your procedure and your healing. Proof opens February 2027; you can plan and prepare with us now, but an inquiry is not yet a booked appointment.

Rebuilding the joint you had repaired

After orthopedic surgery, recovery is its own project — restoring motion, rebuilding strength, and returning to the tasks you need, at a pace set by your surgeon’s protocol and your own healing. Every step is measured, so progress is something you can see rather than guess at.

Who this is for

Within the services Proof plans to offer at opening, post-surgical rehabilitation is for people recovering from common orthopedic procedures:

  • Knee and hip replacement
  • ACL and other ligament reconstruction
  • Rotator cuff and labral (shoulder) repair
  • Spinal surgery — fusion, discectomy, laminectomy
  • Fracture fixation and tendon repairs

If your procedure isn't listed, ask — we'll tell you honestly whether it fits our scope or point you toward better-matched care.

Your recovery is specific to your procedure

Restrictions, when rehab starts, how it progresses, and the goals that matter are different for a joint replacement than for a ligament repair or a spinal surgery. The overview below is orientation — not a treatment protocol to follow on your own. Your operative details, your surgeon's precautions, and your clinician's assessment set your actual plan.

How rehabilitation differs by procedure (general orientation — your surgeon's protocol governs the specifics)
Procedure typeEarly focusWhat varies most
Joint replacement (knee, hip)Restoring motion and controlling swelling early, then progressive strengthening and walking mechanicsWeight-bearing status and milestone timing, set by your surgeon; knee and hip progress differently
Ligament & shoulder repair (ACL, rotator cuff, labrum)Protecting the repair first, then graded motion and carefully progressed loadingHow long the area is protected, when motion begins, and progression — driven by tear/graft, repair type, and surgeon preference
Spinal surgery (fusion, discectomy, laminectomy)Protecting the surgical site, posture and body mechanics, and graded return to activityBending/lifting/twisting precautions and timelines, which depend on the procedure and your surgeon

Because these differ so much, we don't publish a universal recovery calendar, loading schedule, or clearance thresholds. For how published guidance is structured, see AAOS OrthoInfo's staged total knee replacement exercise guide, the APTA knee-ligament clinical practice guideline, and a review of rehabilitation after rotator cuff repair, which describes how protocols vary with tear size, repair type, and surgeon preference.

Preparing — before surgery and before your first visit

Before surgery (if you can)

A pre-surgical ("prehab") evaluation records your starting strength and motion so your recovery targets are concrete, and it's a chance to learn what the early days will ask of you. Ask your surgeon whether prehab fits your timeline.

Bring to your first rehab visit

  • Your operative report and your surgeon's post-op protocol or precautions
  • Any weight-bearing or motion restrictions you were given
  • A current medication list
  • Insurance and referral details (if your plan requires a referral)

We'll confirm the right way to send clinical records with you directly — please don't upload operative reports or protocols into website forms, and we won't ask you to use a records channel until it's set up and confirmed with your office.

What your first evaluation involves

We review your operative report and your surgeon's protocol, then establish protected baselines appropriate to your procedure — swelling, available motion, and muscle activation (for example, quadriceps after a knee procedure or rotator-cuff activation after a shoulder repair). From there we map the criteria your plan progresses through, and explain the plan in plain language. You leave with a written plan you keep.

The stages of rehabilitation (orientation)

1

Protect & restore motion

Within your surgeon's precautions, calm swelling and recover the motion the next stage depends on.

2

Rebuild strength

Progress loading gradually, guided by how the tissue is tolerating it — never faster than your protocol allows.

3

Retrain function

Rebuild the specific tasks you need back — stairs, lifting, overhead reach, or, where relevant, return to sport.

Visit frequency usually starts higher and tapers as you take over more of your own program. Progression is governed by criteria and your clinician's judgment, not the calendar alone.

How we measure progress

Your plan targets a specific activity you want back — climbing your stairs, lifting a grandchild, returning to a sport. We set an appropriate baseline at the start, re-measure at planned points, and use the change to shape the next conversation about your plan. No single number decides when you're done: discharge and return-to-activity are clinical decisions made with you, weighing the measures alongside your circumstances and your surgeon's guidance.

Working with your surgical team

Post-surgical care works best when the clinic and the surgeon's office stay in sync. Our planned workflow, in place from opening: we follow your surgeon's protocol and precautions, and we plan to share objective progress updates with the surgical team at agreed milestones and flag anything off-track early. The exact reporting cadence is confirmed with each referring office.

Evidence & limitations

Structured, supervised rehabilitation is a well-established part of recovery after major orthopedic surgery, and published guidance is procedure-specific rather than universal — see the APTA and AAOS resources linked above. Honest limits apply: tissue healing sets a floor on timelines that no clinic can shortcut; the right starting point and pace are set by your surgeon's protocol and your own healing, not a fixed rule; and performance on any given test day is one input among several, not a verdict. We report what was measured, note the limitations, and never push loading faster than your protocol allows.

Questions, answered

How soon after surgery should I start?

When your surgeon's protocol says — which varies widely. Some procedures (like many knee replacements) begin rehab within days; repairs that need protection start at a surgeon-set date. We work from your protocol, not a generic timeline. A pre-surgical evaluation, when it fits, makes your targets concrete.

Does insurance cover post-surgical rehab?

It depends on your specific plan's physical-therapy benefit — which is separate from what covered your surgery. Coverage turns on network participation, any authorization required, your visit allowance, and your cost share. We verify your rehabilitation benefits before you start and put your expected cost in writing. See our insurance page.

Can you coordinate with my surgeon?

Yes — that's part of the plan. We follow your surgeon's protocol and precautions and plan to send objective progress updates to the surgical team at agreed milestones, flagging anything off-track early. The exact cadence is confirmed with your surgeon's office.

Will you follow my surgeon's exact protocol?

We build your plan around it and the precautions it sets. Where your response to rehab suggests a change, we discuss it with you and your surgeon's office rather than improvising.
