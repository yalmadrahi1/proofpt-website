Services · Specialty Rehabilitation

Gait & Mobility Training

Walking is measurable, trainable, and worth training — speed, symmetry, endurance, confidence.

Home › Services › Gait & Mobility Training

What it is

Gait training is structured rehabilitation of walking: after surgery, injury, or neurological events, and amid the slow erosion that age or deconditioning brings. Walking speed is such a strong predictor of health outcomes that researchers call it the sixth vital sign — and it responds to training.

We treat walking as the measurable skill it is: baseline speed and pattern, targeted correction of the deficits driving the problem, and re-testing that shows the change.

Who it helps

  • Limping or asymmetry after injury or surgery
  • Slowed walking speed and reduced endurance
  • Shuffling, unsteadiness, or fear on uneven ground
  • Walker-to-cane-to-independent progressions
  • Gait changes from neuropathy or neurological conditions

Post-surgical patients normalizing their walk, older adults losing ground or confidence, people progressing off assistive devices, and anyone whose walking has quietly shrunk their world.

How we assess

Measured gait speed, observational and video-assisted pattern analysis, strength and range testing of the links in the chain, and endurance baselines. The limp has a cause; the evaluation names it.

What treatment looks like

Component correction (the hip strength, ankle motion, or balance deficit underneath the pattern), practice on real-world surfaces and stairs, assistive-device progression done on criteria, and endurance building.

Evidence & limitations

Task-specific gait training improves walking speed and capacity across post-surgical, geriatric, and neurological populations — walking improves by walking, structured and progressively challenged. Limits: severe joint damage or progressive disease can cap outcomes, and we set targets from your measured baseline, not a brochure.

Questions, answered

I walk fine, just slower. Does it matter?

Walking speed predicts independence, hospitalization risk, and long-term health better than almost any simple measure — and it is trainable. Slower is a signal worth acting on early.

When can I stop using my walker?

When measured balance and strength say so — progression off a device on criteria is safer and usually faster than either white-knuckling without it or defaulting to it forever.

Can you fix a limp I've had for years?

Often improved, sometimes fully — it depends on the driver. Long-standing patterns take longer to rewrite than fresh ones, which the evaluation will scope honestly.
